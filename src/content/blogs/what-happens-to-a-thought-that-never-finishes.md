title: "What Happens to a Thought That Never Finishes"

---

There's a version of your inner life that existed before constant connectivity became a fixture of every day. Not a purer version, just a different one, where thoughts had more room to unfold before the next interruption showed up.

That version hasn't vanished. It's just been crowded, most of the time, by something faster.

---

The most obvious change is speed. Thoughts that once took minutes to mature now often rush toward a conclusion in seconds. Reactions can arrive before the feeling underneath them has even been named. Once that pace becomes familiar, anything slower can start to feel inefficient, even a little suspicious.

But quick isn't the same as deep. A fast mind can look impressive and still be thin underneath.

---

One of the harder shifts to notice is a growing intolerance for feelings that don't resolve cleanly. Not because those feelings are bad, but because sitting with them takes patience, and a lot of daily life now trains minds to expect resolution on demand.

That friction, the discomfort of not quite knowing yet, isn't a malfunction. It's often exactly where real thinking starts. A mind that's lost some tolerance for ambiguity has lost access to a meaningful part of its own intelligence, because insight tends to show up in the waiting, not before it.

---

This also blurs the line between what you're actually feeling and what you're reacting to. When a mind is constantly fed by outside material, it gets harder to separate a genuine response to your own life from a reaction to whatever just scrolled past.

None of this needs a dramatic fix. It tends to come back gradually, the same way it left: through an environment that gives a thought enough room to actually finish before the next one arrives.
