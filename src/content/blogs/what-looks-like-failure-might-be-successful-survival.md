There's a difficult but useful idea worth sitting with: what looks like dysfunction is often just the shape of survival under conditions that shifted faster than a person could adjust to comfortably. What looks like numbness may simply be a way of staying intact inside a world that asks for a lot, quickly.

A person adapting to pressure isn't the same thing as a person breaking under it.

---

Adapting well doesn't necessarily mean choosing the ideal environment. It usually just means choosing whatever is survivable within the one you're actually in. If stimulation stays constant, sensitivity tends to blunt a little. If comparison is relentless, self measurement tends to organize around whatever's available to compare against. None of this is really accidental. It's more like a living response to sustained pressure, doing exactly what it's built to do.

---

The tricky part is when adaptation starts getting mistaken for identity. A shape that developed under strain can start to feel like a final, permanent shape, when really it was just a response, one that made sense given the conditions at the time. That distinction matters, because what helped someone survive one environment can become genuinely hard to sustain somewhere else.

A person can be highly adaptive and still quietly misaligned with what they actually need. Sometimes that's exactly what misalignment looks like from the inside: not obvious failure, but successful adjustment to the wrong conditions.

---

This is also part of why change can feel so slow. A pattern that once offered protection doesn't release easily, even once the original threat has shifted. Simply understanding that intellectually is rarely enough on its own.

What seems to help more is patience: providing a steadier environment over time and letting the system notice, gradually, that it doesn't need to brace quite as hard anymore. Not a single insight. A slow accumulation of proof.
