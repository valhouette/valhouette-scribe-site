---
title: "Informed Is Not the Same as Clear"
description: "You can be processing more information than any generation in history and still feel strangely unmoored. Here's one reason why."
publishDate: 2024-08-08

---

Not all distortion works by telling you something false. Some of it works simply by arranging what's true in a certain order. What arrives first, what lingers, what gets repeated, what quietly never shows up at all.

A person caught inside that kind of arrangement rarely feels deceived. Most of the time they feel informed. Which is exactly what makes it hard to notice.

---

Information can be technically accurate and still mislead through context. A steady stream of true things, arranged a certain way, can make it genuinely difficult to see the larger pattern they're part of. Over time, this can leave a person more reactive than clear, and, oddly, more confident in a partial view than the situation warrants.

There's a kind of exhaustion that comes from constantly trying to sort out what's actually true in an environment that tends to reward speed over understanding. It's worth having a name for that: it's just tiring, and being tired isn't the same as being wrong.

---

This pattern doesn't stay external either. Once a mind gets used to selective framing from the outside, it can start doing the same thing to itself: highlighting certain feelings, quietly ignoring others, building a story out of whichever evidence is easiest to reach.

Urgency can start to feel like importance. Familiarity can start to feel like truth. A strongly felt conclusion can start to feel like a well reasoned one, even when it hasn't actually been examined.

---

None of this calls for suspicion of everything. Suspicion without discernment is just another distortion wearing a different outfit.

What seems to help more is something slower: reading a little more carefully, holding fewer assumptions, noticing what's missing rather than only what's present, and being a little more patient with conclusions that arrive too quickly and too cleanly. A mind that can tolerate not knowing yet tends to be a harder one to steer.
