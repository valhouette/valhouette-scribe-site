Your hand moves before you are fully conscious.

Not because something urgent is waiting. Not because you made a decision. The hand moves the way a compass needle moves. Automatically. Before you have formed a single thought about the day. Before you have noticed the light coming through the curtains. Before your body has had a chance to remember what it feels like to simply be awake.

Think carefully about what that is, precisely. Think about what you are handing over in that moment.

The first input of your day, before you have had a single thought of your own, is a stream assembled by something that does not know you. It knows your behavioral profile. It knows what you clicked on last Tuesday at 11pm. It knows which emotional register kept your thumb still for half a second longer than usual. It does not know what you are carrying today. It does not know what your mind was reaching for when it surfaced from sleep.

It does not care. It has an inventory to move.

And you spend the rest of the morning slightly displaced. Slightly behind yourself. Not quite sure why.

---

There is a version of waking up that most people alive right now have almost entirely lost access to.

It is not the nostalgic version. It is not candlelight and birdsong and a leather-bound journal. It is simply the version where the first few minutes of consciousness belong to you. Where your mind surfaces on its own. Follows its own thread. Arrives before the world begins making demands of it.

Those early minutes have a function. The mind uses them to orient. To find its own frequency before the day imposes one. When you bypass them with a feed, you skip the arrival. You go from sleep directly into someone else's current. And something in you knows it, even if you cannot name it yet.

---

Try a single experiment. Not forever. Not as a commitment.

One morning. Leave the phone face-down until you have been awake for twenty minutes.

Notice what the first twenty minutes actually contain when nothing is filling them. Notice what thought arrives on its own. Notice whether the discomfort you feel in those minutes is the discomfort of boredom, or something else entirely.

There is a difference between a mind that has nothing to engage with and a mind that has been interrupted before it could find out what it wanted to engage with.

One of those is a problem. The other is what mornings became.

You already know which one you have been living.
