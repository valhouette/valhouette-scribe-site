---

You know the words by heart, the way you know a song heard across many years.

You have never said them aloud.

When you imagine doing so, you hear your own voice set against the recorded one, and you stop.

---

A recorded voice is a gift. It can teach pronunciation, rhythm and shape. It can keep a person company when they have run out of words of their own. Someone else's prayer, offered or shared, is not an empty thing.

But it also sets a standard that no ordinary voice meets: trained, unhurried, certain. Measured against it, your own attempt can feel like an intrusion. Access has done something it never intended. It has made taking part feel like trespass.

---

There is a quieter reason the words stay unspoken. Saying something commits you in a way that listening does not. The words are in the room. They are in your mouth. You can hear them. A voice that has said something is accountable to it.

That may be exactly why they are avoided.

---

Not every practice asks for speech, and no listener is obliged to pray aloud or invent a prayer. Some traditions give listening its own weight. Some are built around silence. Each has its own understanding of words, and those understandings deserve to be learned from the inside.

The question is narrower. Within the practice you have actually chosen, what does taking part ask of you? And is the distance between you and it really belief, or only the sound of an imperfect sentence?

---

If speech belongs in your practice, the first attempt will probably feel awkward. That awkwardness is not evidence the act is false.

It may simply be what doing feels like after a long time of hearing.
