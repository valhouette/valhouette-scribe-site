title: "Reachable Does Not Mean Received"
description: "You can be in contact with everyone you know and still feel like nobody actually caught what you were carrying."
publishDate: 2024-07-04

---

There's a specific kind of loneliness that only shows up when you're constantly reachable. Messages arrive fast. Reactions arrive fast. And somewhere underneath all of it, something in you is still waiting for a form of contact that doesn't look like a notification.

Being in touch with everyone is not the same as being known by anyone in particular.

---

Older forms of communication assumed you were actually there with the other person. Not just visible to them, but present in a way that wasn't edited or scheduled for efficiency. A lot of digital contact quietly swapped that out. It made reaching someone constant and made real depth optional.

You can appear emotionally available to a hundred people and still be structurally absent from all of them.

---

Think about what a like or a quick reply actually costs the person sending it. Almost nothing. No time carved out, no risk taken, nothing revealed about themselves. And yet your nervous system can read that tiny signal as something closer to nourishment, because it's shaped like connection even when it doesn't carry much weight.

Real communication tends to cost something: attention that isn't split, time that isn't compressed, a willingness to stay with someone past the point where it's convenient. That's a lot to ask of a day built around fast exchanges.

---

If you've ever felt strangely lonely with a phone full of unread messages, that might not be a personal failure. It might just be a mismatch between how much contact you're receiving and how much of it actually reaches the part of you that needs to be met, not just messaged.

A pause in a conversation doesn't have to mean something has gone wrong. Sometimes it's just space. Try letting one sit a little longer than usual this week and see what happens on the other side of it.
