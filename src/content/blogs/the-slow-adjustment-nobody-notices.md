---

Picture a quiet room on an ordinary afternoon. Nothing is demanding anything from you. Someone who loves you is nearby. No crisis, no obvious loss.

And still, barely anything registers. No particular warmth, no fullness in the moment. Just a flat, gray kind of spacing out that sits between you and everything around you.

That's a strange thing to try to describe to someone, because it doesn't look like sadness or burnout from the outside. Life still functions. Work gets done. And yet something has thinned.

---

One way to think about this: your baseline for what counts as "enough" stimulation can move without you noticing it moving. Not in one event. In accumulation. In thousands of small moments where the bar shifts up just slightly, then a little more.

That kind of shift is almost impossible to catch while it's happening. Most people don't feel themselves drifting. They feel normal, because the change happened gradually enough to never announce itself. The evidence only shows up later, in a quiet room, when ordinary life doesn't land the way it used to.

---

Try this small experiment. Think of the last time you sat in real stillness. No podcast, no scroll, no phone quietly waiting in your hand. How long ago was that? How long did it actually last?

That answer tends to say more about where your baseline currently sits than most self-assessments would.

---

None of this is a diagnosis. It's more like an observation worth holding lightly: a peaceful evening can start to feel underpowered, and a person you love can start to feel emotionally far away, not because anything between you has changed, but because your own capacity to receive ordinary life has been recalibrated by something else entirely.

The good news buried in that observation is that a baseline that moved gradually can also move back, the same way it moved away: gradually, with repetition, not all at once.
