title: "Living Inside the Interruption"
description: "At some point interruption stops being an event and starts being a place you live. Here's how that shift happens, quietly."
publishDate: 2024-07-29

---

There's a point where interruption stops being something that happens to your attention and starts being the place your attention actually lives. You check a message, return to a task, open another tab, come back. From the outside, everything still looks intact.

On the inside, something else is happening. Attention is being trained to expect discontinuity as the default state, and once that training sets in, continuity starts to feel unfamiliar rather than restful.

---

This is different from ordinary distraction, which is temporary by nature. What's being described here runs deeper than that. It changes how hard it is to stay with anything long enough for depth to actually form, and depth isn't a nice extra. It's usually where meaning shows up in the first place.

A mind that's been fragmented enough gets very good at moving across the surface of a lot of information without ever really absorbing much of it. Busy, but not necessarily engaged. Informed, but not necessarily any wiser for it.

---

Here's the part that's easy to miss: each individual step in this pattern feels useful in the moment. Checking the phone feels like relief. Scrolling feels like a short rest. What doesn't get felt is the cumulative cost, because repetition tends to make a pattern invisible to the person inside it.

And then there's the strange twist at the end of it. When you finally step away from the feed for a while, the first thing that shows up usually isn't peace. It's restlessness. A vague itch that something else should be happening.

That restlessness isn't proof you need to go back. It's more likely proof that your nervous system has simply learned to expect constant interruption as normal.

---

Letting that restlessness pass without immediately feeding it is, in a small way, how the pattern starts to loosen. Not in one sitting. Over time, with enough repetition in the other direction.
