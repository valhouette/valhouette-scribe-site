There is a thought you are about to have.

Before it becomes words, before you decide how to say it or whether to say it at all, there is a moment of raw formation. An experience taking shape. A reaction becoming itself before it has a name.

Pay attention to what is happening in that moment. Not to the thought you eventually surface, but to the grammar it forms inside. The rhythm of it. The architecture. The register it arrives in before language gets involved.

Because something has been happening in that interior space that almost nobody is talking about, and the reason almost nobody is talking about it is that it arrived dressed as a joke.

---

A few years ago, a significant portion of culturally literate, self-aware people began describing a particular phenomenon as brainrot.

They said it about themselves. They used it as a badge. They posted about their brainrot in the vocabulary of the brainrot, which was itself a brainrot move, which everyone recognized without needing it explained. It was funny. The whole thing was very funny.

It was also a precise diagnostic signal that was read almost entirely incorrectly.

Because the thing being described was not primarily a communication style. It was not how people spoke online or the syntax they deployed for irony or the specific joke formats that required seventeen layers of platform context to decode.

It was a description of how people were thinking before they decided to speak.

The feed had migrated inside. Not as performance. As grammar.

---

The internal monologue is not a neutral space.

It has a structure. A rhythm. A way of holding experience before it becomes expressible. And that structure is not fixed. It adapts to the environment it lives inside for most of its hours.

This is not unique to digital environments. Language has always shaped interior experience. The vocabulary available to a person in any era has shaped the texture of their private thought. This is not new.

What is new is the speed of the shaping, and the narrowness of what is doing the shaping. Not a whole culture. Not a tradition or a literature or a shared history. A specific set of content formats, engagement loops, and emotional registers engineered for a particular commercial purpose.

And those formats are now running inside people's heads. Not just on their screens.

---

This is worth sitting with seriously rather than deflecting with irony.

Your internal monologue is one of the few places that has historically been entirely your own. The private space before speech. The room underneath language where you actually process being alive. Whatever happens there determines what you are capable of noticing, caring about, deciding, and becoming.

If that space has been colonized by someone else's architecture, the implications go further than a communication style.

The question is not whether you can spot platform syntax in your own speech. It is whether you can locate a mode of thinking, somewhere inside you, that predates the feed. A way of holding experience that was not handed to you by an algorithm.

It is still there. Under the noise. Under the borrowed grammar.

You will have to be quiet for a while to find it.
